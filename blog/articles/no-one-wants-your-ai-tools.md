No one wants your AI tools.

They applaud the demo, ask for access, and tell you that what you built is exactly what their company needs.

Then they open the platform and have no idea what to do.

We discovered this painfully at deco.

We built an AI platform where companies can connect their data, create agents, give them tools, and organize the context they need to work. The product is powerful. I use it every day. Our finance, sales, operations, and engineering teams use it to do things that would have sounded like science fiction two years ago.

We tried to sell the platform.

It did not work.

## A powerful tool creates work for the customer

A horizontal AI platform arrives empty.

Someone inside the company still needs to decide which problem deserves attention, connect the right data, teach the agent how the business works, define its limits, evaluate its judgment, and take responsibility when it fails.

The tool may make execution 100 times easier. The customer must still supply the hardest part.

Most companies are nowhere near ready for that.

They are trying to run an e-commerce operation, serve patients, approve credit, manage inventory, or sell this season's collection. They do not spend all day thinking about context layers, MCPs, agent orchestration, and autonomous workflows.

We do.

For a long time, I saw that difference as an education problem. So we kept working on onboarding, documentation, examples, and a more polished interface.

But every new screen was another way of asking the customer to design their own solution.

That was our mistake.

## Sell the work

The change was simple: we stopped asking customers to use our platform and started using it for them.

Instead of selling an AI platform to an e-commerce company, we diagnose its digital operation. We find where conversion is leaking, where repetitive labor is consuming the team, and where the site is quietly losing money. Then we put people and agents to work on those problems.

We create the agents, learn the operation, connect the data that matters, and become responsible for producing the result. The customer can judge us on what changed in their business instead of trusting a promise that AI will eventually make them more efficient.

This completely changed the conversation.

Software budgets are small and brutally contested. Right beside them sit much larger budgets for agencies, outsourced operations, media, and repetitive labor. We were fighting for a thin software subscription while our customers were spending an order of magnitude more to get the actual work done.

The opportunity was beside us the whole time.

Our platform became more valuable when we stopped putting it at the center of the sale. It now gives our service an unfair advantage: we can operate faster, preserve context, automate what traditional agencies repeat manually, and turn what we learn in one engagement into capability we can reuse.

Customers experience the result before they need to understand the machinery.

## AI made tools abundant

Making software used to be expensive. A company that built a useful tool owned something scarce.

AI crushed much of that scarcity.

Features that demanded an engineering team can now be reproduced in days. Every model provider is adding agents, tools, memory, connectors, and workflows. A startup selling a generic AI interface is competing with companies that have more money, more distribution, and the foundation model underneath the interface.

The scarce part moved. Very few companies have deep knowledge of a customer's operation, the judgment to decide what matters, and enough trust to take responsibility for a result.

When you sell a tool, the promise is: "Here is what you can do." We started selling a much stronger promise: "We will make it happen."

There is a much larger budget behind that sentence.

## Service is how the product learns

As product people, moving into services felt like surrender.

We wanted to build once and sell infinitely. We wanted customers to sign up, understand the product, and reach value without speaking to us. Every hour of implementation looked like evidence that the software was incomplete.

I no longer believe that.

In a market this new, service is the fastest path to discovering what the product must become.

Every engagement forces us to face the ugly details that a horizontal demo hides. Which permissions block the agent? Which action requires human approval? What context changes the quality of its judgment? Where does automation create value, and where does it merely create activity?

You cannot learn those things from a blank workspace and a signup funnel.

By doing the work, we discover the loops that repeat. Then we encode them into agents, tools, evaluations, and interfaces. What begins as expert service gradually becomes software.

The product becomes the accumulated memory of outcomes we have already produced.

## Earn self-service

I still believe our platform can become globally self-service.

But we tried to skip the part where we earn it.

Customers will configure agents themselves after we know exactly which agents they need. They will trust the system to operate their business after we run it beside them and understand every reason it might fail. Onboarding only becomes a product flow after someone performs it manually enough times to find what actually matters.

The service is our activation energy.

It generates revenue now, puts us close to serious problems, and builds the domain context that will make the software defensible later.

If customers keep opening your AI platform and asking what they should do with it, stop explaining the platform.

Choose a painful problem, solve it with the tools you built, and put your name behind the result.

After you have done that enough times, customers will finally want the product. The empty box of possibilities will have become a proven way to get something important done.
