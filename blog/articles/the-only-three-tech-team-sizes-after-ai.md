I believe tech teams after AI come in only three useful sizes: 2, 10, and 30.

With two people, you can prove an idea. Ten can build a high-growth application. Thirty can carry a platform and the services around it.

The numbers in between are usually transitions. Above thirty, you are looking at a collection of teams, and each one should be able to explain why it needs to exist.

After nine years at VTEX and now building deco, I have become much less impressed by headcount. Large teams can produce extraordinary results, but size itself tells me almost nothing. Sometimes it only reveals how much coordination a company has learned to tolerate.

AI makes that tolerance much more expensive.

## Two people prove an idea

One person can build a product now. That is no longer controversial.

One founder with agents can write the code, design the interface, create the landing page, analyze feedback, and ship changes in the same day. Work that once demanded an early team can happen before lunch.

I still would not build a company alone.

The second person is not there because the AI needs help writing code. They are there because the founder needs someone who can challenge the idea, speak to customers, notice self-deception, and carry the emotional weight of creating something from nothing.

AI can replace a frightening amount of labor. It cannot stand beside you and declare, with equal commitment, that the future you see deserves to exist.

For a proof of concept, two is enough. One of them does not need to be a technical cofounder. Both need to be capable of turning a customer conversation into a product decision, and both need to use AI as part of their basic work.

At this size, you are searching for truth.

Can you make the thing? Does anyone care? Will someone use it twice? Will someone pay?

Until those answers are clear, adding people mostly increases the cost of being wrong.

## Ten people build a high-growth application

When the product works, the problem changes.

You need to improve it while customers are using it. Reliability begins to matter. Distribution can no longer be the founder sending messages one by one. Support becomes a daily source of product knowledge. Someone must keep the whole experience coherent while demand pulls it in ten directions.

Ten excellent people can do all of this for an application.

The exact job titles matter less than the capabilities inside the room. The team needs product judgment, engineering depth, design, distribution, customer obsession, and operational discipline. Several people must carry more than one of those.

Agents absorb much of the execution that used to justify specialized departments. They write migrations, test edge cases, produce documentation, investigate incidents, analyze calls, and prepare campaigns. Humans remain responsible for context, standards, taste, and decisions.

Ten is large enough to have range and small enough for everyone to understand the product.

You can still put the whole company around one table. A customer problem can travel from support to product to production without crossing three management layers. The founders can remain close enough to reality that strategy does not become a presentation about work happening somewhere else.

That closeness is an enormous competitive advantage.

## Thirty people build a platform and its services

An application can remain beautifully focused. A platform accumulates responsibilities with every person who builds on it.

Platforms carry other people's businesses. They need primitives, infrastructure, security, observability, documentation, integrations, developer experience, and support for use cases the original team did not invent.

The moment you add services, another kind of work appears. Someone must understand each customer's domain deeply enough to help them get a result, then convert that knowledge into reusable product capability.

Thirty people give you enough surface area to do both.

Some people will spend most of their time protecting the core or expanding the platform. Others must stay painfully close to customers and bring back the context that keeps the roadmap honest. Agents make what they learn reusable across the company.

At this size, leadership becomes organizational design. The founder can no longer be the router for every decision, but the company is still small enough to share one context and one standard of quality.

Beyond thirty, I stop seeing a team. I see a portfolio.

There may be good reasons to have 300 or 3,000 people. A global company needs sales, legal, finance, local operations, and many product lines. But its technology organization should decompose into units that resemble these same shapes: pairs exploring, teams of ten growing applications, and groups of thirty operating platforms.

## AI punishes coordination without purpose

The old justification for headcount was execution capacity. More roadmap required more engineers.

That relationship is breaking.

The bottleneck is moving toward deciding what matters, carrying enough context to make good decisions, and holding a standard across everything agents produce. Adding people does not automatically increase any of those. Past a certain point, it often makes them worse.

The useful constraint is to build the smallest team capable of carrying the whole problem. Fashion has nothing to do with it.

For the companies being created now, that number is usually 2, 10, or 30.
