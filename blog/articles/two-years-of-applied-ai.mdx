The race stopped being about which model you use. It is about who builds the factory around it.

The last two years look like a parade of buzzwords. They are not. Each one solved the thing the previous one broke, and none of them was worth much on its own. Here is the chain.

<FactoryTimeline locale="en" />

## Prompt engineering: we got better at asking

You type a question, you get an answer. If the answer is bad, you rewrite the question. That was the whole job in 2023 — Anthropic advertised a Prompt Engineer role in January that year at [$250k to $335k](https://web.archive.org/web/20230118233623/https://jobs.lever.co/Anthropic/e3cde481-d446-460f-b576-93cab67bd1ed), and eight weeks later the same posting said $175k.

It stopped being a job when the models stopped needing the trick. OpenAI's documentation now tells you that ["prompting them to 'think step by step' or 'explain your reasoning' is unnecessary."](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning-best-practices)

But you were still there for every single answer.

## Tools: the model learned to ask for things

June 2023, and this is the move everything else is built on. OpenAI shipped function calling: ["describe functions to gpt-4-0613 and gpt-3.5-turbo-0613, and have the model intelligently choose to output a JSON object containing arguments to call those functions."](https://openai.com/index/function-calling-and-other-api-updates/)

Read what that does. The model does not look anything up. It stops mid-sentence and emits a request — *call get_order_status with this order ID* — and waits. Your code decides whether to honour it, runs it, hands the answer back.

That is still true. An agent has no hands; it asks, and something you control does the work. One call, then it stopped and waited for you. But the factory has hands now, and this is them.

## Agentic workflows: it stopped answering and started working

March 2024, Andrew Ng: ["I think AI agentic workflows will drive massive AI progress this year — perhaps even more than the next generation of foundation models."](https://x.com/AndrewYNg/status/1770897666702233815)

Ng's point was that you could chain those requests. Instead of one call and a stop, the model plans, calls a tool, reads what came back, and calls another. It loops.

He was right, and it was the first time anyone said out loud that the machine around the model was the point. The factory has a production line now.

Then every step it took made something else obvious: it knew nothing about your business.

## Context engineering: we showed it our business

November 2024, Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol. It opened on a plumbing problem: ["Every new data source requires its own custom implementation, making truly connected systems difficult to scale."](https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-context-protocol)

That is what finally made tool calling catch. Function calling had been available since June 2023, and for the next year and a half every connection was hand-built — one integration per tool, per vendor, per customer. MCP made it one protocol, and it spread.

Seven months later the practice got its name, and it came from a merchant rather than a lab. Tobi Lütke: ["the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM."](https://x.com/tobi/status/1935533422589399127)

The context is the specific stuff. Your catalog rules. Your merchandising logic. The one checkout exception for the São Paulo warehouse, and why it exists. Documentation debt stopped being untidy and became a line item.

That gave the factory two things: pipes into your systems, and some idea of what matters inside them.

Now it could tell you exactly what to do. It still could not do it.

## Harness engineering: we let it act

By late 2025 the missing piece was not knowledge. It was permission.

A harness is what the agent is allowed to touch: the terminal, the repository, your admin API, your error tracker. An agent with those can do work. An agent without them is an expensive colleague who may only give advice.

This part is already finished as a differentiator — thirteen months after Anthropic published MCP it was [handed to the Linux Foundation](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/linux-foundation-announces-the-formation-of-the-agentic-ai-foundation) with AWS, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI on the board. You still have to lay the pipes into your own systems, and nobody wins on it.

Every worker on the floor now has a defined reach, which is the difference between a factory and an accident. But it still started every morning from zero and worked out the same things again.

## Memory: we let it keep the answer

This is the part that turns a loop that repeats into a loop that improves, and it is the one most companies are missing right now.

MIT went looking for why enterprise AI pilots stall, and the answer was not compute or regulation or talent: ["The core barrier to scaling is not infrastructure, regulation, or talent. It is learning. Most GenAI systems do not retain feedback, adapt to context, or improve over time."](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf) The most frequent complaint they recorded, ahead of everything else, was "it doesn't learn from our feedback." A lawyer in that study described a $50,000 contract tool that repeats the same mistakes and needs the whole context typed in again every session.

Fixing that works. In a paper accepted to ICLR this year, an agent that writes down what worked and carries the playbook forward went from [42.4 to 59.4 on AppWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04618) — with 75% fewer trial runs and 83% less spend. A smaller open model with an accumulating playbook matched the top production agent.

The industry is selling this as "context graphs." Be careful: the category is a year old, the firm that named it concedes the capability does not fully exist yet, and on plain fact lookup a knowledge graph [loses to ordinary retrieval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05690) at forty times the tokens. The data structure is not the point. Keeping the answer is.

That is the factory's records room, and it is the reason the thousandth job costs less than the first.

And it still sat there waiting to be told what to work on.

## The software factory: we gave it a goal

Everything above makes the machine better at doing what it was told. A factory changes who tells it.

I am not the only one calling it that. Factory.ai described the same thing in June: ["The software factory starts with signals from the outside world: bug reports, internal conversations, customer feedback, business requirements. Monitoring that deployed software generates more signals. The entire system is a continuous feedback loop."](https://factory.ai/news/software-factory) BCG put it in a report in March: agents build, test and ship around the clock, humans define the business intent and review the outcomes.

<FactoryPlant locale="en" />

Put the parts where they go. Tools are the hands. The loop is the production line. MCP is the pipework into your systems, and context is knowing what runs through it. The harness is what each worker may touch. Memory is the records room. None of that is a factory until you add the last thing: a goal, and someone accountable for it.

The inputs are not tickets. They are signals: a supplier feed lands with broken images, a competitor drops price on your top SKUs, Tuesday's deploy costs you conversion by Thursday. The system watches, decides what is worth building, ships it, tests it on live traffic, keeps the answer, and goes again.

## One missing part is enough to get nothing

That chain is not a menu. Context with no harness is advice you execute by hand. A harness with no context is an agent confidently changing the wrong thing. Memory with no loop is a database nobody reads. A goal with none of the above is a slide.

Fix the model and change only the machine around it: a controlled test found a [28-point swing](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08529) between the best and worst scaffold on identical weights, and stripping the tools from a coding agent costs [up to 45% of its performance](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.25873). A weaker model with a better machine now beats a stronger model with a worse one.

This is why 95% of AI pilots produce no measurable result. Most of them bought one part.

**Today** matters in that sentence. Anthropic's own guidance says to ["find the simplest solution possible"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents), and serious people expect this advantage to shrink as models improve. It may. It has not yet.

## The model is not the differentiator anymore

Opus 5 landed on 24 July, GPT-5.6 on 9 July, Grok 4.6 two days ago. Two numbers matter more than any of that.

<FactoryGap locale="en" />

On raw intelligence, the best open model and the best closed model are basically tied. Sixty against sixty-three.

Give them an actual job — multi-step work in a terminal, the kind an agent does all day — and it is 58.7 against 83.8.

Same models. The closed labs ship the machine around the model. The open weights arrive as just a model. You can buy the intelligence for almost nothing now. The machine is the part you have to build.

## What the numbers say about money

You have been promised a lot. Here is what is measured.

The St. Louis Fed read 490,000 earnings calls and found [95% of AI productivity talk is in the future tense](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2026/jul/ai-productivity-what-firms-say-earnings-calls). The Atlanta Fed asked 750 CFOs what AI did for them, then computed it from their own numbers: they [said 1.8% and their figures implied 0.6%](https://www.atlantafed.org/-/media/Project/Atlanta/FRBA/Documents/research/publication/working-paper/2026/03/25/04-artificial-intelligence-productivity-and-the-workforce-evidence-from-corporate-executives.pdf). Three times more felt than measured — remember that when a vendor shows you a case study.

Now the part I did not expect. That same study found the firms with real gains did not get them by cutting costs — cutting labour and cutting other costs were the two lowest-ranked motives on the list. The gains came from new products and serving customers better. And where MIT found real savings, they came from cancelling BPO contracts and cutting agency spend by 30%, "without material workforce reduction."

So the money is not in firing your team. It is in the invoices you send outside, and in revenue you could not reach before.

On revenue, the signal is already in your data: Adobe measured AI-referred retail visits converting [54% higher](https://business.adobe.com/resources/sdk/.q3-ai-traffic-trends-report/q3-2026-ai-sourced-traffic-insights.pdf) than everything else and growing 138% a year, having been worth less than half a normal visit twelve months ago. Two caveats — that traffic is still a fraction of a percent of sessions, and retail is the slowest sector to adopt any of this, [14% of firms](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html) against 19.8% across the economy. The demand arrived before the operation did.

## Building one is the new race

You will not win this on model choice. That decision has a shelf life of about six weeks and everyone in this room will make roughly the same one.

You win it on how fast you can assemble the whole chain: something that watches your operation, knows your business, is allowed to act, keeps what it learns, and runs against a goal you set.

We are building exactly that for digital experience, at [decocms.com](https://decocms.com/?utm_source=vibegui.com&utm_medium=article&utm_campaign=two-years-of-applied-ai).

The people who spend the next year comparing models will have a spreadsheet. The people who spend it assembling the chain will have a factory.
