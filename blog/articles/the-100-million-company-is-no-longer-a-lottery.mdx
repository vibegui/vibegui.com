I believe you can build a $100 million company with 100% certainty.

Choose a service that companies already outsource for between $3,300 and $33,000 per month. Hire the best 5% of the people who already know how to do the work. Give them AI. Make the service twice as good or ten times faster without changing the price. Then keep at it for 24 months.

The startup game is no longer a lottery.

That sounds irresponsible. Startup outcomes are supposed to follow power laws. Most companies fail, a few survive, and one global category winner returns the fund.

That model made sense when the game was to invent a software category and capture the world before someone else did.

AI-native services are a different game. You do not need to predict new demand. The demand is already there. The budget is approved. The customer already trusts an external company to perform the work.

You only need to replace the company doing it.

## The arithmetic is almost boring

Assume a company producing $20 million in annual recurring revenue can be sold for five times revenue.

That is a $100 million company.

$20 million ARR means roughly $1.67 million in monthly recurring revenue.

Agencies, consultancies, business-process outsourcers, managed service providers, accountants, recruiters, brokers, and specialized operators already charge exactly this kind of money. You are not asking a customer to create a new experimental AI budget. You are taking responsibility for an outcome they already purchase every month.

Julien Bek made the opportunity painfully clear in Sequoia's [Services: The New Software](https://sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software/): for every dollar companies spend on software, they spend six on services.

## Replace the vendor, not the employee

The best starting point is work that has already been outsourced.

When a company outsources something, it has accepted three important facts: the work can happen outside the company, there is already a budget for it, and the provider can be judged by the outcome.

Replacing an employee requires a reorganization. Replacing an outsourced provider requires a better proposal.

This is why the opportunity is much more predictable than building another AI tool. A tool creates work for the customer. They still need to understand the tool, redesign the process, train the team, supervise the output, and accept responsibility when it fails.

An AI-native service says: give us the work. We will make it happen.

The customer does not need to understand agents, context engineering, evaluations, model routing, or MCP. They only need to see that the result is better.

## The experts are already here

You also do not need to train an entire new workforce.

Every service industry already has exceptional people who understand the work at a depth no foundation model can reproduce from a prompt. They know which exceptions matter, what customers forget to mention, where quality quietly collapses, and which decisions require judgment earned over years.

Hire the best 5% of them.

Then give each one the capacity that previously required a team.

The expert teaches the system how the work actually happens. The system preserves that process intelligence, executes the repeatable parts, checks its own output, escalates uncertainty, and improves every time the company delivers the service.

This is the company operating system: the industry's process intelligence uploaded into agents, tools, interfaces, approvals, and evaluation loops.

One extraordinary professional with AI should be able to produce what twenty average professionals produced before, with more consistency and less waiting. The company needs drastically fewer people to serve the same revenue. That is where the margin comes from.

## The first six months should be slow

The first six months will not look like a software rocket ship.

You need somewhere between two and twenty customers. Eight is enough for the math.

At first, your experts will still perform too much of the work manually. Good. That is how you learn what deserves to become an agentic loop, where human approval is essential, which context changes the answer, and how the customer can adopt the service without reorganizing their company around your technology.

Trying to automate the service before serving the customer is another form of hiding.

Sell the outcome. Perform the work. Find the repetitions. Encode them. Repeat.

By month six, you need a service customers renew, a delivery system that gets better with every engagement, and enough proof to make distribution repeatable.

Then the curve changes.

Start with eight customers and grow 3x, 3x, 2x, and 2x over the next four quarters:

You are now 18 months in.

You have six months left to go from 288 customers to 500. That is 74% total growth across two quarters—hard, but far less violent than what you already did.

At an average of $3,300 per month, 500 customers put you around $20 million ARR.

The enterprise version follows the same logic with fewer, larger contracts. Fifty customers at $33,000 per month produce almost the same result.

## What I mean by 100% certainty

Markets remain uncertain. Customers leave. Competitors move. Teams make mistakes. A strategic buyer may refuse to pay five times revenue. Selling a private company is never literally guaranteed or liquid.

The certainty comes from no longer depending on a miracle.

You do not need millions of people to discover a new behavior. You do not need to invent a category, become the default product globally, or get lucky with a distribution channel before it closes.

You choose an existing budget. You deliver better work. You retain the customer. You turn what your experts learn into software. You keep going for 24 months.

Most founders will still fail because keeping at anything for 24 months with this degree of focus is brutally difficult. They will change the niche, rebuild the platform, chase a new model, or try to automate judgment they have not earned.

The condition is not "have a good idea."

The condition is to keep producing the result.

## This opportunity will be local first

The last software cycle rewarded global category winners. If you started in San Francisco, studied at Harvard, or lived inside another global tastemaker network, you had an insane advantage. You could see the new behavior earlier, recruit the people naming the category, and distribute the product through the same network.

That advantage continues to exist.

But AI-native services do not require a global big bang.

Services will remain lubricated by humans. Trust is local. Language is local. Regulation, relationships, taste, and the ugly exceptions inside a business process are local.

Brazil already has accounting firms, agencies, recruiters, brokers, consultancies, and outsourcing companies collecting these budgets. So does Mexico. So does Indonesia. So does every meaningful economy in the world.

Each local service industry can produce its own $100 million AI-native companies, predictably and repeatedly.

For the first time in a long time, a founder outside the global tastemaker regions does not need to win a worldwide software category to build something meaningful and scalable.

They can start with work their neighbors already pay someone else to do.
